Toric lenses and astigmatism at cataract surgery

ClarityIQ · Reviewed by a board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 18, 2026.

During cataract surgery, the cloudy lens inside your eye is replaced with an artificial lens. If your eye has astigmatism, your surgeon may discuss a toric version of that lens. A toric is not a fourth lens category. It is an add-on that can be built into a monofocal, an extended depth of focus (EDOF), or a multifocal (full-range) lens.

This page will not tell you whether to choose a toric lens. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon. Ask the same questions no matter which lens type you are considering. You can use this page at any point before the lens is implanted — before your consult, after it, or as a second look.

What astigmatism is

Astigmatism means the front of the eye is shaped more like a football than a basketball. That uneven curve can blur vision at every distance, not only up close or only far away.

Regular astigmatism has an even, repeating shape that a toric lens is designed to match. Irregular astigmatism is uneven, and a toric may not be the right tool. Your surgeon's measurements, not this page, show which kind you have and how much.

What a toric lens is designed to do

A toric lens is designed to correct regular astigmatism inside the eye. If your eye has enough regular astigmatism to benefit, that blur is treated at the lens. This applies whether you want low dependence on glasses or you are fine wearing them. Glasses, if you wear them, often work better because there's less for them to fix.

The same framework for every lens type

A toric does not change the basic design of the lens type it is added to. The goals and trade-offs of a monofocal, EDOF, or multifocal still apply. The extra points to weigh are whether your astigmatism is a good match and whether you accept the chance that the lens could rotate. Each category below uses the same three prompts: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and a question to take to your surgeon.

Monofocal with a toric

What it is designed to do
The monofocal's chosen focus — far, arm's length, or near — plus correction of regular astigmatism.
Trade-offs
You will still need glasses for the distances the monofocal is not set for. The toric has to stay lined up, and it can rotate.
A question to ask
If we use a monofocal, could a toric version help, and what would I still need glasses for?

EDOF with a toric

What it is designed to do
One focus stretched from far into arm's length, plus correction of regular astigmatism. Readers are still common for small print.
Trade-offs
The trade-offs of EDOF lenses still apply, including some halos or dim-light change for some people. The toric has to stay lined up, and it can rotate.
A question to ask
For the EDOF lenses you use, is a toric version available, and how would it change what I need glasses for?

Multifocal with a toric

What it is designed to do
More than one focus — far, arm's length, and near — plus correction of regular astigmatism.
Trade-offs
Halos and glare are more common with multifocal lenses. The toric has to stay lined up, and it can rotate.
A question to ask
For the multifocal lenses you use, is a toric version available, and what trade-offs should I expect?

The halo and glasses trade-offs of each category are covered in more detail in Monofocal vs multifocal vs EDOF lenses.

When a toric may not help

Not every eye benefits. If your astigmatism is small, irregular (an uneven shape on the front of the eye), or mainly in the back of the cornea, a toric may not help, or may not be the right tool. An irregular cornea or a condition such as keratoconus can also limit which lenses will help.

The measurements taken before surgery matter. A dry or uneven surface can throw off those measurements, so the surface is often treated first and then measured again. Ask your surgeon whether your eye has enough regular astigmatism to benefit. A fuller list of eye conditions that can change the plan is in Your eye decides what's possible.

Alignment and rotation

Surgeons plan the alignment of a toric lens carefully. It has to be lined up at an exact angle. If a toric lens rotates out of position, it corrects less of the astigmatism, so your vision may not be as good as planned. A lens that rotates can sometimes be repositioned.

Other ways astigmatism is sometimes treated

A toric lens is one way to correct astigmatism inside the eye. In some eyes, other options can include corneal incisions at the time of surgery, a light-adjustable lens whose power can be adjusted after surgery, or leaving the astigmatism for glasses to handle. Ask your surgeon which approach fits your eye. This page does not rank those options.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology's preferred practice pattern for cataract in the adult eye asks surgeons to counsel people about elective options such as astigmatism management and specialty lenses, including toric and postoperatively power-adjustable lenses. Source: Miller et al., Ophthalmology, 2022, PMID 34780842. Light-adjustable lens: FDA PMA P160055.

Questions to bring to your consult

  1. Do I have astigmatism? Is it regular, and is there enough for a toric lens to help?
  2. What other ways could we treat it?
  3. Can a toric version be used with each lens type we are discussing?
  4. What happens if the lens rotates, and what would adjusting it involve?
  5. Do my eye-surface measurements need to be repeated first?

Frequently asked questions

What is a toric lens?

A toric lens is a cataract lens designed to correct regular astigmatism inside the eye. It is an add-on, not a fourth lens category. Toric versions exist for monofocal, EDOF, and multifocal (full-range) lenses.

Do I need a toric lens?

This page cannot answer that. Whether a toric will help depends on how much regular astigmatism your eye has, and on your surgeon's exam. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon.

Can I get a toric version of a monofocal, EDOF, or multifocal lens?

Toric versions exist in all three categories. Whether one will help depends on how much regular astigmatism your eye has, not only on which category you are considering.

What happens if a toric lens rotates after surgery?

If a toric lens rotates out of position, it corrects less of the astigmatism, so your vision may not be as good as planned. Surgeons plan the alignment carefully, and a lens that rotates can sometimes be repositioned. Ask what that would involve in your case.

Are there other ways to treat astigmatism at cataract surgery?

Yes. In some eyes, options can include corneal incisions, a light-adjustable lens, or leaving the astigmatism for glasses to handle. Ask your surgeon which approaches fit your eye. This page does not tell you which to pick.

Does ClarityIQ tell me whether to choose a toric lens?

No. ClarityIQ explains what a toric lens is designed to do and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
