How to choose a cataract lens: a plain-language guide

Written by Martin de la Presa, MD, a board-certified cornea, cataract and refractive surgeon. Last updated November 17, 2026.

During cataract surgery, the cloudy lens inside your eye is removed and replaced with an artificial lens. This new lens is called an intraocular lens, or IOL. Before surgery, you'll be asked to choose which kind of lens you want. The lens is meant to stay in your eye for the rest of your life, so it helps to understand your options before your visit.

This guide explains each lens type in plain words: what it's designed to do, what it trades away, and what to ask. It won't tell you which lens to pick. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon.

One honest rule for every lens below: the goal of cataract surgery is vision that is better than you have now. With any lens, you may still need glasses for your best vision.

Start with one question: what do you want to see without glasses?

Doctors talk about three ranges, or focal points:

  • Distance: driving, TV, road signs, faces across the room.
  • Intermediate (arm's length): a computer screen, a car dashboard, cooking.
  • Near: a phone, a book, a menu, small print.

Think about your normal day. Which ranges matter most to you? How much would it bother you to wear glasses for some tasks? How much do you drive at night? Your answers to these questions shape most of the lens decision.

Each lens type, in plain words

Monofocal lens

What it's designed to do: A monofocal lens is set for one distance, usually far. You can expect better vision than you have now at that distance, though you may still need glasses for your best vision there. For the other ranges, like arm's length and reading, you'll need glasses.

Trade-offs: The final focus can land a little off target, and a monofocal lens doesn't correct astigmatism. Either one can blur vision at the chosen distance. Glasses usually take care of that. A monofocal lens is a well-established, complete option. Choosing it is a real decision, not a fallback.

Monovision

What it's designed to do: Monovision is a plan, not a separate lens. One eye is set for distance and the other eye for near. Together, the two eyes cover more of the range. You can expect better vision than now, though you may still need glasses for your best vision, such as for night driving or long reading.

Trade-offs: Each eye on its own misses the opposite end, and depth perception can be a little lower. Some people adjust easily and some never do. If you've never tried it, ask whether you can test it with contact lenses before surgery. A milder version, called mini-monovision, is also an option to ask about.

Toric lens (for astigmatism)

What it's designed to do: Astigmatism means the front of the eye is shaped more like a football than a basketball. It can blur vision at every distance. A toric lens is designed to correct astigmatism at its focal point, so you can expect better vision than now there, though you may still need glasses for your best vision. Toric versions exist for several lens types.

Trade-offs: The lens has to be lined up at an exact angle. Uncommonly, it turns slightly after surgery, and your surgeon may need to adjust it. It's worth asking how your surgeon handles that.

Extended range lens (EDOF)

What it's designed to do: Extended depth of focus lenses are designed to cover distance and extend into arm's length. Many people can see a computer screen or a dashboard without glasses. You can expect better vision than now across that range, though you may still need glasses for your best vision.

Trade-offs: Reading glasses are still common for small print. Halos or glare at night are possible, usually milder than with full range lenses.

Full range lens (multifocal or trifocal)

What it's designed to do: Full range lenses are designed to cover distance, arm's length and near. Many people can do most of their day (driving, computer, phone) without glasses. Some still keep readers for small print or dim light. Your surgeon can tell you how likely that is for your eyes.

Trade-offs: Halos or glare around lights at night are more common. Some people notice a little less contrast in dim light. For many people, halos become much less noticeable over the first few months as the brain adapts. A small number keep noticing them, which is worth asking about.

Light-adjustable lens

What it's designed to do: This lens can be fine-tuned after surgery. Once your eye has healed, a special UV light treatment adjusts the lens power, and a final treatment locks it in. You can expect better vision than now at the range you and your surgeon choose, though you may still need glasses for your best vision.

Trade-offs: It takes several extra visits. You'll wear UV-protective glasses from surgery until the lens is locked in. Some medicines and eye conditions rule it out, so your surgeon will check.

What about cost?

Coverage varies by lens type and by insurance plan. Ask your surgeon's office for the cost of each option, per eye, in writing.

Things about your eyes that can change your options

Your eyes matter as much as your wishes. Astigmatism, dry eye, macular or retina problems, glaucoma, past LASIK and the health of the cornea can all change which lenses are possible. That's why your measurements and exam matter more than anything you read online, including this page.

Questions to bring to your consult

  1. Which lens types are possible for my eyes, and why not the others?
  2. Do I have astigmatism? How would it be corrected?
  3. With each option, what will I still need glasses for?
  4. What are the night-vision trade-offs with each option?
  5. What does each option cost me, per eye?
  6. If the result isn't what I hoped, what are my options afterward?

Frequently asked questions

Is there one lens that's right for everyone?

No. Each lens type is designed for different goals, and each trades something for something else. Which one is right for you depends on what you want to see without glasses, the health of your eyes, and your surgeon's exam.

Will I still need glasses after cataract surgery?

Maybe. With any lens, you can expect vision that is better than you have now, though you may still need glasses for your best vision. With a monofocal lens, you'll need glasses for the ranges it isn't set for. Full range lenses are designed so many people can do most of their day without glasses, but some still keep readers.

Does insurance cover cataract lenses?

Coverage varies by lens type and by plan. Ask your surgeon's office for the cost of each option, per eye, in writing.

Can the lens be changed later?

Replacing a lens is possible, but it means another surgery with its own risks. It's best to think of your choice as permanent.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which lens to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains what each lens type is designed to do and its trade-offs, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.

This page is education, not medical advice. It doesn't diagnose anything or tell you which lens to choose, and it doesn't create a doctor-patient relationship. Your surgeon decides with you, based on your exam.
