How to choose a cataract lens: a plain-language guide

Written by Martin de la Presa, MD, a board-certified cornea, cataract and refractive surgeon. Last updated November 17, 2026.

During cataract surgery, the cloudy lens inside your eye is removed and replaced with an artificial lens. This new lens is called an intraocular lens, or IOL. Before surgery, you'll be asked to choose which kind of lens you want. The lens is meant to stay in your eye for the rest of your life, so it helps to understand your options before your visit.

This guide starts with two questions about your goals, then explains the three lens categories, then shows how those goals and categories fit together. A later section gathers the trade-offs — including halo, glare and contrast in dim light — with figures from FDA studies. It won't tell you which lens to pick. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon.

One honest rule for every lens below: the goal of cataract surgery is vision that is better than you have now. With any lens, you may still need glasses for your best vision.

Start with your goals

Before you look at lens categories, it helps to sit with two questions together. Your answers, plus the health of your eyes, shape most of the lens conversation with your surgeon.

1. How much do you want to depend on glasses?

  • Low: you'd like to do most of your day without them.
  • Don't mind: glasses are fine either way.
  • Prefer glasses to handle the rest: you'd rather wear glasses for some tasks and keep the lens simpler.

2. Which distances matter, and which matters most?

  • Far (distance): driving, watching TV, reading road signs, or seeing faces across the room.
  • Arm's length: a computer screen, a car dashboard, or a cutting board.
  • Near: a phone, a book, a menu, or small print.

It also helps to note how much you drive at night, because that ties to the trade-offs in a later section.

Any of the three categories can be set for a single distance. A monofocal lens can be targeted for far, for arm's length, or for near. That is why a simple table that says "arm's length means EDOF" or "near means full range" is misleading. What usually matters is the combination of those two questions, not one distance on its own.

The 3 lens categories

Cataract lenses fall into three categories. Each is designed for different goals, and each trades something for something else. No category is better than the others in every situation. The right fit depends on your goals and your exam. Trade-offs for each category are gathered in the Trade-offs section below.

Monofocal lens (including enhanced monofocal)

A monofocal lens is set for one distance, usually far. You can expect better vision than you have now at that distance, though you may still need glasses for your best vision there. For the other ranges, like arm's length and reading, you'll need glasses.

This category also includes enhanced monofocal lenses, which are still monofocals, not a separate category. They are designed to stretch a little into arm's length. You'll still need glasses for near, and often for some arm's-length tasks.

A monofocal lens is a well-established, complete option. Choosing it is a real decision, not a fallback.

Extended range lens (EDOF)

Extended depth of focus lenses are designed to cover distance and extend into arm's length. Many people can see a computer screen or a dashboard without glasses. You can expect better vision than now across that range, though you may still need glasses for your best vision. Reading glasses are still common for small print.

Full range lens (multifocal or trifocal)

Full range lenses are designed to cover distance, arm's length and near. Many people can do most of their day (driving, computer, phone) without glasses. Some still keep readers for small print or dim light. Your surgeon can tell you how likely that is for your eyes.

Matching goals to categories

This section is information, not a recommendation. No category is ranked, and no single lens is featured. The branches below start from your glasses goal, then take into account which distances matter most.

If your eye has meaningful astigmatism

Astigmatism means the front of the eye is shaped more like a football than a basketball. It can blur vision at every distance. A toric version of a lens is how that astigmatism is corrected inside the eye. Toric versions exist in every category: you can have a toric monofocal, a toric EDOF or a toric full-range lens.

If your eye has enough regular astigmatism to benefit, correcting it inside the eye is how that blur is treated at the lens. This applies whether you want low dependence on glasses or you are fine wearing them. Glasses, if you wear them, often have less left to fix because some of the astigmatism has already been addressed in the eye.

Not every eye benefits. If your astigmatism is small, irregular (an uneven shape on the front of the eye), or mainly in the back of the cornea, a toric may not help, or may not be the right tool. A toric also has to be lined up at an exact angle. It can rotate slightly after surgery, which can reduce the correction, and your surgeon may need to adjust it. Ask your surgeon whether your eye has enough regular astigmatism to benefit, and how they handle rotation.

If you want low dependence on glasses

These goals usually involve an EDOF or full-range lens, or monovision: one monofocal eye set for far and the other for near or arm's length. Which of those fits depends on which distances matter most to you, and on how much halo, glare and contrast change you would accept. Those trade-offs are in the next section.

If you don't mind wearing glasses either way

A monofocal lens (toric if your eye has enough astigmatism to benefit) keeps the optics simpler. EDOF, full-range and monovision are still options if you want them.

If you prefer glasses to handle the rest

A monofocal set for your most important distance (toric if your eye has enough astigmatism to benefit) is the usual match. You would wear glasses for the other distances.

What about cost?

Cost and coverage differ by lens and by plan. Ask your surgeon's office for the cost of each option, per eye, in writing.

Trade-offs

There is a third question to sit with, alongside your glasses goal and your distances:

How much halo, glare and loss of contrast in dim light would you accept for more range without glasses?

These effects are real, but they should not be overblown or minimized. In FDA studies, many people noticed some rings or rays around lights. Most were not very bothered. A smaller share found them very bothersome. The exact shares vary by lens design and by how the question was asked, so the figures below come from specific FDA studies, not from one combined average. People in the monofocal comparison groups also reported some halos and glare, just usually fewer.

Monofocal, including enhanced monofocal

Halo, glare and starbursts. Of the three categories, monofocal lenses had the lowest rates in these studies, but the rates are not zero. In the monofocal group of one full-range FDA study, about 23% of people noticed some halo (77% reported none), and fewer than 1% were "bothered very much" by halos, starbursts or glare.

Source: AcrySof IQ PanOptix SSED, P040020/S087, Tables 20–21, 6 months, monofocal control arm.

Contrast in dim light. In these studies, the monofocal lens is the comparison point. US labels for enhanced monofocals say the effect of their optical design on contrast and visual disturbances has not been evaluated in clinical trials, so this page does not give separate percentages for them.

Other. The final focus can land a little off target. Glasses usually take care of that. A monofocal lens does not correct astigmatism unless it is a toric version.

Extended range (EDOF)

Halo, glare and starbursts. This varies by design, so one number would be misleading.

  • In one EDOF FDA study, 1% to 2% of people reported very bothersome starbursts, halos, blurred vision or dark-area symptoms, and no one in the study had the lens removed for this. Severe ratings were close to the monofocal group: starbursts 3.8% vs 2.7%, halos 0.9% vs 0.9%, glare 0% vs 0%.
  • In another EDOF FDA study, 6.8% of people were "very bothered" by halos (vs 2.7% with the monofocal), 8.2% by starbursts (vs 4.1%), and 5.5% by glare (vs 3.4%).
  • In a third EDOF FDA study, spontaneous reports of halos, night glare and starbursts were 4.4% to 5.3%, close to the monofocal group (0.9% to 4.5%), and no one in the EDOF group reported those symptoms as severe.

Sources: AcrySof IQ Vivity SSED and FDA information brochure, P930014/S126, Table 21 and p. 51; TECNIS Symfony SSED, P980040/S065, Table 18; TECNIS PureSee SSED, P980040/S176, p. 31 and Table 22.

Contrast in dim light. This also varies by design. The official label for one EDOF lens says most people who receive it are likely to have a significant loss of contrast compared with a monofocal, and should take care in dim light, including when driving at night. Another EDOF FDA study found no clinically meaningful difference in contrast from the monofocal control.

Sources: Vivity directions for use / labeling, P930014/S126, Warnings; PureSee SSED, P980040/S176, p. 57.

Other. Reading glasses are still common for small print.

Full range (multifocal or trifocal)

Halo, glare and starbursts. More people notice rings or rays around lights than with a monofocal. In some studies, most of those people were not very bothered. In others, the share who were very or extremely bothered was higher.

  • In one full-range (trifocal) FDA study, about 64% of people noticed some halo, compared with about 23% in the monofocal group. Fewer than 5% said they were "bothered very much" by halos (2.4%), starbursts (4.8%) or glare (1.6%). The study text says most people described these as "not bothered at all" to "bothered somewhat." Severe ratings were higher than "bothered very much": halos 13%, starbursts 16%, glare 3%.
  • In the FDA study of another full-range lens, about 82% of people experienced halos when asked directly. 3.8% were "extremely bothered" and 9.9% were "very bothered." 5.3% were "extremely bothered" by starbursts. 8.1% had a symptom that interfered with daily activity for more than three months; one person in that study had the lens removed.
  • In a third full-range FDA study, 37% of people had halos "quite often or very often," compared with 7% in the monofocal group. 80% described their halos as "not at all" to "a little" bothersome; 7% called them very bothersome, and 6% called them severe.

Sources: PanOptix SSED, P040020/S087, Tables 20–21 and FDA information brochure Tables 4–5; TECNIS Synergy SSED, P980040/S124, Tables 16–17 and p. 32; enVista Envy SSED, P240005, pp. 29 and 37.

Contrast in dim light. Full-range lenses often measure a bit lower than a monofocal in dim light, though the size of that difference is not the same in every study. One study called the binocular difference "not clinically meaningful" (and did not test each eye on its own). Another found contrast "generally lower," especially in high glare. A third found a meaningful difference favoring the monofocal in most test conditions. Several labels also say to take extra care when driving at night.

Sources: PanOptix SSED, P040020/S087, p. 21; Synergy SSED, P980040/S124, p. 28; Envy SSED, P240005, p. 29; and the corresponding FDA information brochures or directions for use.

Other. Some people still keep readers for small print or dim light.

Other trade-offs that aren't their own category

Toric (an add-on, available in each category)

Halo, glare and contrast. A toric version uses the same optical category as the non-toric lens it is based on. The extra trade-off is alignment, not a new kind of halo profile.

Other. The lens has to be lined up at an exact angle. It can rotate slightly after surgery, which can reduce the astigmatism correction, and your surgeon may need to adjust it. Official labeling for one toric lens notes that rotation off the intended axis can reduce the correction, and that a misalignment greater than 30 degrees may increase remaining astigmatism. In one FDA rotational study of a monofocal lens, 98.4% of people stayed within 10 degrees of the planned position at 6 months (mean change 2.27 degrees); one eye rotated 40 degrees after suspected trauma. That is one study, not a rate for every toric lens. Ask your surgeon how they handle rotation.

Sources: Vivity toric labeling, P930014/S126, Warnings; Clareon SSED rotational sub-study, P190018.

Monovision (a setup, usually with monofocals)

Monovision is a setup, not a lens category. One eye is set for distance and the other for near, or sometimes for arm's length (a milder version called mini-monovision). Together, the two eyes cover more of the range. You can expect better vision than now, though you may still need glasses for your best vision, such as for night driving or long reading.

Halo, glare and contrast. Because this is usually done with monofocal lenses, halo and glare rates follow the monofocal category more than the full-range category. The extra trade-offs are about how the two eyes work together.

Other. Each eye on its own misses the opposite end, and depth perception can be a little lower. Some people adjust easily and some never do. If you've never tried it, ask whether you can test it with contact lenses before surgery.

A specific rate for the change in depth perception was not found in the FDA documents reviewed for this page.

Light-adjustable (a monofocal technique, not a category)

A light-adjustable lens is a monofocal that can be fine-tuned after surgery. It uses a different technique, not its own category. Once your eye has healed, a special UV light treatment adjusts the lens power, and two final treatments lock it in. You can expect better vision than now at the range you and your surgeon choose, though you may still need glasses for your best vision.

Halo, glare and contrast. This page does not give a separate bothersomeness percentage for this technique from the FDA study, because a clean comparison table was not extracted for this draft.

Other. It takes several extra visits (adjustment treatments, then two lock-in treatments). You'll wear specified UV-protective glasses during all waking hours from surgery until 24 hours after the final lock-in. Some medicines and eye conditions rule it out — including some medicines that increase sensitivity to UV light, some that are hard on the retina, macular disease, a history of ocular herpes simplex, and nystagmus — so your surgeon will check.

Source: Light Adjustable Lens SSED and professional labeling, P160055, Indications, Contraindications, and UV-protective eyewear instructions.

Things about your eyes that can change your options

Your eyes matter as much as your wishes. Astigmatism, dry eye, macular or retina problems, glaucoma, past LASIK and the health of the cornea can all change which lenses are possible. That's why your measurements and exam matter more than anything you read online, including this page.

Questions to bring to your consult

  1. Which lens categories are possible for my eyes, and why not the others?
  2. Do I have astigmatism? Is it regular, and is there enough for a toric lens to help?
  3. With each option, what will I still need glasses for?
  4. What are the halo, glare and dim-light trade-offs with each option?
  5. What does each option cost me, per eye?
  6. If the result isn't what I hoped, what are my options afterward?

Frequently asked questions

Is there one lens that's right for everyone?

No. Each lens category is designed for different goals, and each trades something for something else. Which one is right for you depends on what you want to see without glasses, how much halo, glare or contrast change you would accept, the health of your eyes, and your surgeon's exam.

Will I still need glasses after cataract surgery?

Maybe. With any lens, you can expect vision that is better than you have now, though you may still need glasses for your best vision. With a monofocal lens, you'll need glasses for the ranges it isn't set for. Full range lenses are designed so many people can do most of their day without glasses, but some still keep readers.

Do all lenses cause halos and glare?

No. People in monofocal groups in FDA studies also reported some halos and glare, just usually fewer, and very few were very bothered. Full-range lenses have higher rates of noticing rings or rays around lights. In some studies most people were not very bothered; in others the share who were very or extremely bothered was higher. EDOF results vary by design. Ask your surgeon how that applies to the lenses you are considering.

Does insurance cover cataract lenses?

Cost and coverage differ by lens and by plan. Ask your surgeon's office for the cost of each option, per eye, in writing.

Can the lens be changed later?

Replacing a lens is possible, but it means another surgery with its own risks. It's best to think of your choice as permanent.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which lens to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains what each lens category is designed to do and its trade-offs, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
