Far or near? Choosing the focus for a monofocal lens

By Martin de la Presa, MD, Founder, ClarityIQ · board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 23, 2026.

A monofocal lens is designed for one distance. That distance can be far, arm's length, or near. It is a real choice, not a lesser fallback. This page is about that choice — especially if you are nearsighted and you currently read without glasses.

This page will not tell you which distance to aim for. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon. You can use this page at any point before the lens is implanted — before you see your surgeon, after, or as a second look.

At a glance

  • A monofocal can be aimed at far, arm's length, or near.
  • You will still need glasses for the distances the monofocal is not set for.
  • If you are nearsighted and you currently read without glasses, setting both eyes for distance means you will likely need glasses for near work.

What a monofocal is designed to do

A monofocal is a real choice, not a consolation prize. It is designed for one chosen focus. That can be far, arm's length, or near. Whatever you choose, you will still need glasses for the other distances.

The same framework for each focus

Each option below uses the same three prompts: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and a question to take to your surgeon. This page does not rank the three distances.

Far

What it is designed to do
Distance tasks such as driving or seeing a TV across the room.
Trade-offs
You will likely need glasses for near work, and often for some arm's-length tasks.
A question to ask
If both eyes are aimed at far, what will I still need glasses for?

Arm's length

What it is designed to do
A computer screen, a car dashboard, or a cutting board.
Trade-offs
You will likely need glasses for far, and usually for small print at near.
A question to ask
If we aim at arm's length, what will I still need glasses for in my day?

Near

What it is designed to do
A phone, a book, a menu, or small print.
Trade-offs
You will likely need glasses for distance, including driving.
A question to ask
If both eyes are aimed at near, what will I still need glasses for, including night driving?

If you are nearsighted and you read without glasses now

You should not assume that distance is the right aim. The natural lens that gives you near vision today is the one being removed, so the question is what you want your new focus to be, not what you have now. If a monofocal is aimed at distance, you'll likely need glasses to read. If it is aimed at near, you can often keep reading without glasses, but you'll need glasses for distance. This page does not tell you which to pick.

If you are nearsighted and you currently read without glasses, setting both eyes for distance means you will likely need glasses for near work. A monofocal can be aimed at far, arm's length, or near, so you can talk about keeping that near vision if it matters to you.

Enhanced monofocal lenses

This category also includes enhanced monofocal lenses, which are still monofocals, not a separate category. They are designed to stretch a little into arm's length. You'll still need glasses for near, and often for some arm's-length tasks. Ask your surgeon how the specific lens you are considering would behave at each distance.

Other ways people cover more distances

People who want lower dependence on glasses often discuss an EDOF lens, a full-range (multifocal) lens, or monovision. This page does not rank those options. Each one has its own trade-offs, including glasses, halos and glare, and, with monovision, depth perception.

Monovision is a setup, usually with monofocal lenses, not a lens category. One eye is set for distance and the other for near, or sometimes for arm's length. That is different from aiming both eyes at the same distance, which is what the rest of this page describes.

Want a fuller explanation of monovision, mini-monovision, and depth perception? See Monovision with cataract surgery. For the three lens categories, start with the lens guide and the side-by-side comparison. If night halos and glare are on your mind, see Halos and glare after cataract surgery.

Questions to bring to your surgeon

  1. If we use a monofocal, should we aim at far, arm's length, or near, given how I spend my day?
  2. I am nearsighted and I read without glasses now. If both eyes are set for distance, what happens to that near vision?
  3. What would I still need glasses for with each of those targets?
  4. How is aiming both eyes at the same distance different from monovision?
  5. Should we also talk about an EDOF or full-range lens, or about monovision?

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean to aim a monofocal at far or near?

A monofocal is designed for one distance. Aimed at far, it is meant for distance tasks such as driving, and you will likely need glasses to read. Aimed at near, you can often read without glasses, but you will need glasses for distance. Aimed at arm's length, it is meant for things like a computer screen, and you will likely need glasses for far and for small print. This page does not tell you which to pick.

I'm nearsighted and read without glasses now, so I should pick distance, right?

Not necessarily. The natural lens that gives you near vision today is the one being removed, so the question is what you want your new focus to be, not what you have now. If a monofocal is aimed at distance, you'll likely need glasses to read. If it is aimed at near, you can often keep reading without glasses, but you'll need glasses for distance. This page does not tell you which to pick.

What about arm's length?

Arm's length is a computer screen, a car dashboard, or a cutting board. A monofocal can be aimed there, just as it can be aimed at far or at near. You will likely still need glasses for the other distances. Ask your surgeon what that would mean in your day.

Will I still need glasses with a monofocal?

Yes, for the distances the monofocal is not set for. What you would still need glasses for depends on which distance is chosen and on your surgeon's exam. This page cannot answer that for you.

How is this different from monovision?

This page is about aiming both eyes at the same distance. Monovision is a setup in which one eye is set for distance and the other for near, or sometimes for arm's length. They are different plans. See Monovision with cataract surgery if that is also on your list.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which focus to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains what a monofocal is designed to do at each distance, and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
