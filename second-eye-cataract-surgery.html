Planning the second eye in cataract surgery

By Martin de la Presa, MD, Founder, ClarityIQ · board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 23, 2026.

Cataract surgery is done one eye at a time, even when both eyes need it. The plan for the second eye is part of the lens conversation: when it is done, how you will manage in between, and whether the result in the first eye should change anything about the second.

This page will not tell you how far apart to do the two eyes, or whether both eyes should match. Those choices belong to you and your surgeon. You can use this page at any point before the second lens is implanted — before you see your surgeon, after the first eye, or as a second look.

At a glance

  • Most people who need surgery in both eyes have the two eyes done on separate days. The interval is a decision you and your surgeon make together.
  • The wait lets the first eye settle, gives time to check for early problems, and can let the first-eye result help guide the second-eye plan.
  • Some surgeons and some systems do both eyes on the same day. That can mean one recovery period, but the first-eye result cannot be used to adjust the second-eye plan, and a rare complication could affect both eyes.
  • In between separate-day surgeries, the two eyes can feel unbalanced, especially if you had a strong glasses prescription. Ask how you will manage during that stretch.
  • The first-eye result can also be a moment to revisit the plan — the target, the lens type, or whether the two eyes should match.
  • For the three lens categories themselves, see our lens guide and the side-by-side comparison.

Why the second eye is part of the plan

The two eyes work together. After the first eye is done, the second eye — and the difference between the two — still shapes how you see at each distance, how comfortable glasses feel, and how balanced things feel in depth. If the second eye also has a cataract that is affecting your vision, surgery there is a separate decision, made with your surgeon, not an automatic next step on a set date.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology's preferred practice pattern lists the things that should shape the timing of second-eye surgery: your visual needs and preferences, how the second eye is functioning, whether the first eye has settled medically and in its focus, the need to restore the way the two eyes work together and to reduce a large focus difference between them, time to check for and treat early problems after the first surgery, and practical matters such as travel. This page does not tell you how to weigh those for you.

Source: Miller et al., Ophthalmology, 2022 (Cataract in the Adult Eye Preferred Practice Pattern), PMID 34780842.

When the second eye is done

The most common approach is to operate on the two eyes on separate days, with enough time for the first eye to settle and for early problems to be checked. How long that interval is varies. It is not a single standard number. Your surgeon may wait until the focus in the first eye is stable enough to learn from, or may schedule the second eye once they are confident the first eye is healing well. Ask what interval they recommend for you, and why.

Some surgeons and some systems do both eyes on the same day, one after the other, often called immediate sequential bilateral cataract surgery. That can mean a single recovery period and no stretch in which the two eyes feel unbalanced. It also means the first-eye result cannot be used to adjust the second-eye plan, and a rare complication could affect both eyes. The preferred practice pattern treats this as an individual decision, made with your surgeon, not as the default in every setting. This page does not tell you which timing to choose.

If the first eye is a light-adjustable lens, the timing of the second eye may need to wait until the light treatments on the first eye are finished. Concurrent light treatments in both eyes were not done in that lens's clinical trial. See The light-adjustable lens at cataract surgery.

Sources: Miller et al., 2022, PMID 34780842; Light Adjustable Lens professional labeling, P160055, Precautions (concurrent light treatments in both eyes were not performed in the clinical study).

Using the first-eye result to plan the second

Lens power is chosen from measurements made before surgery. Even with careful measurements, the final focus can land a little off the planned target. When the two eyes are done on separate days, the result in the first eye can help refine the calculation for the second, because some of the difference may be similar in both eyes.

In a 2011 study of 2,129 people who had delayed surgery in both eyes with the same lens model, the first-eye difference and the second-eye difference tended to run together. When the first eye landed between 0.50 and 1.50 diopters off the planned target, applying half of that difference to the second-eye calculation raised the share of second eyes within 0.50 diopters of target from 56% to 75%. A 2019 study that tested this idea with both older formulas and a newer one found a smaller improvement, especially with the newer method: in a 605-person check set, the share of second eyes within 0.50 diopters of target with that newer formula went from 70.74% to 72.73%. How much this helps depends on the method your surgeon uses and on how similar your two eyes are. The link is weaker when the corneas differ by more than 0.60 diopters. Ask your surgeon whether they use the first-eye result this way.

Sources: Aristodemou et al., Ophthalmology, 2011, PMID 21762991; Turnbull and Barrett, Journal of Cataract & Refractive Surgery, 2019, PMID 31326224. The 2011 figures used the Hoffer Q, Holladay 1, and SRK/T formulas. The 2019 check-set figures quoted above are for the Barrett Universal II formula.

What the wait in between can feel like

When the two eyes are done on separate days, there is a stretch in which one eye has a new lens and the other does not. The two eyes can feel unbalanced, especially if you had a strong glasses prescription before surgery. That imbalance is one reason the interval matters, and it is also why some people ask about same-day surgery.

Options for that stretch can include taking the lens out of your old glasses for the operated eye, or wearing a contact lens in the other eye. What will work depends on your prescription, your eyes, and how long the interval is. Ask your surgeon, before the first surgery, how you will manage in between.

If something feels wrong after the first eye — increasing pain, a sudden drop in vision, worsening redness, or new flashes, floaters, or a curtain in your vision — contact your surgeon rather than waiting for the second-eye appointment. Those can be warning signs that need a prompt check. This is one reason many surgeons leave time between the two eyes.

Revisiting the plan after the first eye

The second eye does not have to be an exact copy of the first. After you have lived with the first eye for a little while, you and your surgeon can look at how that focus is working and decide whether to keep the same target, aim the second eye a little differently, or talk about a different lens type.

If both eyes are being set for the same distance, that is a different plan from monovision, in which one eye is set for distance and the other for near, or sometimes for arm's length. The first-eye result can inform either path. This page does not tell you whether the two eyes should match. See Monovision with cataract surgery and Far or near? Choosing the focus for a monofocal lens if those questions are on your list.

If the first-eye focus landed off the planned target, options for that eye can include glasses or contacts, a laser touch-up on the cornea, an adjustment if you have a light-adjustable lens, or, rarely, exchanging the lens. The second-eye plan can take that into account. Ask your surgeon: if the first eye lands off target, what would we do, and would we change anything about the second eye?

Questions to bring to your surgeon

  1. How far apart will you do my two eyes, and why is that interval right for me?
  2. Is same-day surgery in both eyes an option in your practice, and what would the trade-offs be?
  3. How will I manage in between if the two eyes feel unbalanced?
  4. Will you use the result in the first eye to refine the plan for the second?
  5. Should both eyes be set for the same distance, or is a different plan on the table?
  6. If the first eye lands off the planned target, what would we do, and would that change the second eye?
  7. If a light-adjustable lens is in the plan, how would the light treatments affect the timing of the second eye?
  8. What warning signs after the first eye should make me call you before the second-eye date?

Frequently asked questions

How long should I wait between eyes?

There is no single standard interval. Many people have the two eyes done on separate days, with enough time for the first eye to settle and for early problems to be checked. Some surgeons and some systems do both eyes on the same day. The interval is a decision you and your surgeon make together, based on how the first eye is healing, your visual needs, and other factors. This page does not tell you how long to wait.

Can both eyes be done on the same day?

In some practices, yes. Same-day surgery can mean one recovery period and no in-between imbalance. It also means the first-eye result cannot be used to adjust the second-eye plan, and a rare complication could affect both eyes. Ask your surgeon whether it is offered and whether it is a reasonable option for you. This page does not tell you which timing to choose.

Will the first-eye result change the plan for the second eye?

It can. When the two eyes are done on separate days, some of the difference between the planned focus and the result in the first eye may help refine the calculation for the second. Studies have found that using part of that difference can improve how close the second eye lands to its target, though how much it helps depends on the calculation method and on how similar your two eyes are. The second eye is also a moment to revisit the target or the lens type. Ask your surgeon whether they use the first-eye result this way.

What if the two eyes feel unbalanced in between?

That is common when one eye has a new lens and the other does not, especially if you had a strong glasses prescription. Options can include taking the lens out of your old glasses for the operated eye, or wearing a contact in the other eye. Ask your surgeon, before the first surgery, how you will manage during that stretch.

Do both eyes have to get the same lens?

No. Many people have the same type and the same target in both eyes, but the second eye does not have to be a copy of the first. After the first eye, you and your surgeon can decide whether to keep the same plan, change the target, or talk about a different lens type, including whether the two eyes should match. This page does not tell you which to pick.

What warning signs after the first eye should I watch for?

If you have increasing pain, a sudden drop in vision, worsening redness, or new flashes, floaters, or a curtain in your vision, contact your surgeon rather than waiting for the second-eye appointment. Those can be warning signs that need a prompt check.

Does ClarityIQ tell me how to plan the second eye?

No. ClarityIQ explains why the second-eye plan matters, and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
