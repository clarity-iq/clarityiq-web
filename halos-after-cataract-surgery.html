Halos and glare after cataract surgery: what to expect

By Martin de la Presa, MD, Founder, ClarityIQ · board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 23, 2026.

After cataract surgery, some people notice rings of light around headlights and street lamps, or a glow that can make night driving harder. Those rings are called halos. Glare is a wash of light that can make contrast harder to use, especially at night. This can happen with any lens, including monofocals.

This page will not tell you which lens to choose. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon. You can use this page at any point before the lens is implanted — before your consult, after it, or as a second look. If you already had surgery and you are worried about new symptoms, start with the warning-signs section below and call your surgeon rather than relying on this page.

At a glance

  • Halos are rings of light around headlights and street lamps. Glare is a wash of light that can make it harder to see in dim conditions.
  • Any lens can cause halos and glare, monofocals included. They are more common with full-range (multifocal / trifocal) lenses.
  • Most people notice more halos. Most aren't very bothered by them, and some are. The evidence is mixed from one study to the next.
  • For many people, halos become less noticeable over the first months as the brain adapts, a process called neuroadaptation. Some people stay bothered by them.
  • Ordinary night halos are not the same as warning signs. Call your surgeon right away for vision loss; pain that will not go away even with medicine; a very red eye; or new flashes of light, or a lot of new floaters.
  • For the three lens categories themselves, see our lens guide and the side-by-side comparison.

What halos and glare are

Halos are rings of light around headlights and street lamps. Glare is a bloom or wash of light that can make contrast harder to use, especially at night. Starbursts — streaks that appear to radiate from a light — are sometimes discussed alongside them. Ask your surgeon how each of those might show up with the lenses you are considering.

Any lens can cause them

Any lens can cause halos and glare, monofocals included, and they are more common with full-range lenses. Most people notice more halos; most aren't very bothered by them, and some are. Ask your surgeon how that applies to the lenses you are considering.

What the studies show

Multifocal and trifocal lenses tend to bring more halos and glare. An overview of research and a Cochrane review found halos and glare more common with multifocal lenses than with monofocals. In the trials comparing trifocal and EDOF lenses directly, both caused them, and the trials were too small to show a meaningful difference. One 2022 network analysis found no statistical difference in glare or halos between multifocal and monofocal lenses, so results vary between studies.

Researchers have limited confidence in these findings, and most studies looked at only a few lens models. Ask your surgeon how the specific lenses you are considering compare for eyes like yours.

In one FDA study of a trifocal lens, more people noticed halos than in the monofocal group. Noticing a halo and being very bothered by it are different measures. Sources: PanOptix SSED, P040020/S087, Tables 20–21; de Silva et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2016, PMID 27943250; Tavassoli et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2024, PMID 38984608; Daka et al., British Journal of Ophthalmology, 2025, PMID 40623803; Cho et al., JAMA Ophthalmology, 2022, PMID 36136323. Studies used different questionnaires, so their results should not be ranked against each other.

Neuroadaptation

Over time, the brain often learns to tune them out, a process called neuroadaptation, so for many people halos become less noticeable over the first months. Some people stay bothered by them. This is not a promise, and one trial is not the last word. Ask your surgeon what to expect with the lenses you are considering, and what to do if the symptoms do not settle.

Sources: Zhang et al., 2021, PMID 34194292; Rosa et al., 2017, PMID 29120714.

The same framework for every lens type

A discussion of halos and glare belongs with every category, not only with full-range lenses. Each category below uses the same three prompts: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and a question to take to your surgeon. This page does not rank the categories.

Monofocal

What it is designed to do
One chosen focus — far, arm's length, or near. Halos and glare can still occur.
Trade-offs
You will still need glasses for the distances the monofocal is not set for. Some people notice night rings or glare even with a monofocal.
A question to ask
With the monofocal you use, what should I expect for night halos and glare?

EDOF

What it is designed to do
One focus stretched from far into arm's length. Readers are still common for small print.
Trade-offs
Some people notice halos, glare, or a dim-light change. Contrast in dim light can be lower than with a monofocal for some designs.
A question to ask
For the EDOF lenses you use, what should I expect for halos, glare, and night driving?

Multifocal (full-range)

What it is designed to do
More than one focus — far, arm's length, and near.
Trade-offs
Halos and glare are more common with multifocal lenses. Most people notice more halos; most aren't very bothered by them, and some are.
A question to ask
For the multifocal lenses you use, how often do people notice halos, and how often do they stay bothered by them?

When to call your surgeon

Ordinary night halos around lights are a known optical effect of many lenses. They are not, on their own, the same as the warning signs below.

The National Eye Institute says to call your eye doctor right away if you notice vision loss; pain that will not go away even with medicine; a very red eye; or new flashes of light, or a lot of new floaters (small dark spots or lines that drift across your vision). Ask your surgeon whether that list is complete for you, and what else you should watch for.

Source: NEI, Cataract surgery (updated December 5, 2024).

Questions to bring to your consult

  1. With each lens type we are discussing, what should I expect for halos, glare, and night driving?
  2. How long do those symptoms usually take to fade, and what if they don't?
  3. How would this apply if I drive a lot at night?
  4. What warning signs should I call you about after surgery?
  5. Does anything about my eyes change this picture?

Frequently asked questions

Do all lenses cause halos and glare?

Any lens can cause halos and glare, monofocals included, and they are more common with full-range lenses. Most people notice more halos. Most aren't very bothered by them, and some are. Ask your surgeon how that applies to the lenses you are considering.

Will the halos go away?

Every lens may cause some halos at night. For many people they become less noticeable over time as the brain adapts, a process called neuroadaptation. Some people stay bothered by them. Ask your surgeon what to expect with the lenses you are considering.

Are halos more common with multifocal lenses?

Yes, the usual finding is that multifocal and trifocal lenses tend to bring more halos and glare than monofocals. One 2022 network analysis found no statistical difference, so results vary between studies. Ask your surgeon how this applies to the specific lenses you are considering.

When should I call my surgeon?

The National Eye Institute says to call your eye doctor right away if you notice vision loss; pain that will not go away even with medicine; a very red eye; or new flashes of light, or a lot of new floaters. Ordinary night halos around lights are not, on their own, the same as those warning signs. Ask your surgeon whether that list is complete for you.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which lens to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains what halos and glare are, what the studies show, and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
