Questions to ask your surgeon about cataract lenses

ClarityIQ · Reviewed by a board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 18, 2026.

The lens implanted during cataract surgery is meant to stay in your eye for the rest of your life. These questions can help you feel confident about your options at any point before that happens — before your consult, after it, or as a second look.

This page will not tell you which lens to pick. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon. Ask the same questions about every lens type you are offered.

At a glance

  • Use this list any time before the lens is implanted, not only before the first consult.
  • Ask the same three things about each option: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and what you would still need glasses for.
  • Include your goals, the health of your eyes, measurements, and what happens if the focus lands off the planned target.
  • Write the answers down, or bring a family member who can listen with you.
  • For a fuller explanation of each lens type, see our lens guide and the side-by-side comparison.

Why these questions matter

A useful consult covers more than "which lens do you want?" According to the American Academy of Ophthalmology's preferred practice pattern for cataract in the adult eye, the surgeon should explain the advantages and disadvantages of intraocular lens options, discuss where your focus could be set, consider other conditions in your eyes, and answer your questions. A written list can make that conversation easier to follow.

Source: Miller et al., Ophthalmology, 2022, PMID 34780842.

How to use this list

You do not have to ask every question in one visit. Start with the ones that matter most to you, and keep the rest for a follow-up if you need it. Use the same framework for every lens type you are offered, including types that were not mentioned at first.

This page is about the lens decision. It is not a complete list of medical questions about the operation itself. MedlinePlus and the National Eye Institute also publish question lists that cover preparation, medicines, and recovery.

Your goals

Start here. Your daily life should shape the conversation, not the other way around.

  1. Which daily tasks matter most to me at far, at arm's length, and at near?
  2. How much do I want to depend on glasses, and for which tasks would I be fine wearing them?
  3. I drive at night / I read a lot / I work at a computer. How should that shape the options we discuss?

Which lens types are possible for my eyes

There are three categories: monofocal, extended depth of focus (EDOF), and multifocal (also called full range). Toric lenses, monovision, and light-adjustable lenses are add-ons or setups, not a fourth category. See the side-by-side comparison for what each category is designed to do.

  1. Which of the three categories are possible for my eyes, and why not the others?
  2. For each option, please walk through the same points: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and what I would still need glasses for.
  3. Are there lens types you do not offer, and if so, why?

Focus and glasses

No lens type guarantees life without glasses. The useful question is what you would still need them for.

  1. With each option, what will I still need glasses for — distance, arm's length, or reading?
  2. If we use a monofocal lens, could it be aimed at far, at arm's length, or at near, and what would I need glasses for in each case?
  3. I am nearsighted and read without glasses now. If both eyes are set for distance, will I lose that near focus?

The glasses questions above follow the same points listed by MedlinePlus, "Cataracts — what to ask your doctor" (reviewed October 2, 2024).

Halos, glare, and dim light

Every lens may cause some halos or glare at night. The usual finding is that these are more common with multifocal and trifocal lenses. Evidence on how much they fade is mixed: some people are not much bothered, and some are. Vision can settle over weeks to months, a process called neuroadaptation.

  1. What halos, glare, or dim-light changes should I expect with each option?
  2. How long do they usually take to fade, and what if they still bother me?
  3. I do a lot of night driving. How should we think about that with each type?

Sources: de Silva et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2016, PMID 27943250; Zhang et al., 2021, PMID 34194292; Rosa et al., 2017, PMID 29120714. A longer explanation is in our lens comparison.

Your eyes, measurements, and other conditions

The health of your eyes and the measurements taken before surgery decide what is possible. If you have had LASIK, PRK, or RK, or if you have retina disease, glaucoma, dry eye, or corneal disease, ask how each one affects every lens type, not only one favorite type. A fuller list is in Your eye decides what's possible.

  1. What measurements will you take, and does the surface of my eye need treatment first?
  2. Do I have astigmatism? Is it regular, and is there enough for a toric lens to help? What other ways could we treat it?
  3. Can a toric version be used with each lens type we are discussing?
  4. Would monovision be an option for me, and can I try it with contact lenses first?
  5. Does anything about my eyes — including prior LASIK, PRK, or RK, the retina, glaucoma, the cornea, or dry eye — change which types are a reasonable fit?
  6. Should I tell you about medicines I take, including prostate medicines such as tamsulosin?

A fuller explanation of toric lenses and other ways to treat astigmatism is in Toric lenses and astigmatism at cataract surgery.

The National Eye Institute notes that tests of the size and shape of the eye help the surgeon plan the lens. See NEI, Cataract surgery (updated December 5, 2024). Monovision contact-lens trials: Hayashi et al., 2011, PMID 20839664.

If the focus lands off the planned target

  1. If the focus lands off the planned target, what options would we have?
  2. Is a light-adjustable lens an option for me, and what does the adjustment process involve?

Light-adjustable lens: FDA PMA P160055.

The two eyes

  1. How far apart will you do my two eyes, and how will I manage in between?
  2. Will the result in the first eye change the plan for the second?
  3. If I have a cataract in both eyes, would you ever operate on both on the same day?

The operation and after surgery

These questions are shorter on purpose. This page is about the lens decision. Still, a few items are worth asking before you decide.

  1. Do you use a laser for any part of the surgery, and why?
  2. What warning signs should I call you about after surgery?
  3. If the capsule behind the new lens later becomes cloudy, called posterior capsule opacity (secondary cataract), how would that be treated?

The National Eye Institute says to call your eye doctor right away if you notice vision loss; pain that will not go away even with medicine; a very red eye; or new flashes of light, or a lot of new floaters (small dark spots or lines that drift across your vision). Ask your surgeon whether that list is complete for you, and what else you should watch for.

For more on ordinary night halos versus warning signs, see Halos and glare after cataract surgery. If monovision is on your list, see Monovision with cataract surgery.

Sources: NEI, Cataract surgery (updated December 5, 2024); laser-assisted vs standard cataract surgery, Narayan et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2023, PMID 37369549.

Frequently asked questions

When should I ask these questions?

Any time before the lens is implanted. You can use this list to prepare for your first consult, to follow up after it, or as a second look if you still have questions.

Should I still ask if I already had my consult?

Yes, if the lens has not yet been implanted and you still have questions. A second conversation can help you feel more confident before a permanent choice.

Will my surgeon mind if I bring a written list?

A written list is a reasonable way to use the consult. The American Academy of Ophthalmology's preferred practice pattern for adult cataract asks the surgeon to explain lens options and to answer your questions.

Do I need to ask about every lens type?

Ask the same questions about every type you are offered: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and what you would still need glasses for. You and your surgeon can then decide together.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which lens to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains the options and helps you prepare questions for your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
