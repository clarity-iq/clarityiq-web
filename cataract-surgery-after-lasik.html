Cataract surgery after LASIK, PRK or RK: lens choice

By Martin de la Presa, MD, Founder, ClarityIQ · board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 23, 2026.

If you had LASIK, PRK, or RK years ago, that history belongs in the cataract conversation. Those procedures change the shape of the cornea, which is one of the measurements used to choose the power of the new lens. Lens power is harder to predict after these procedures. This page explains why, what to bring, and the questions to ask.

This page will not tell you which lens to choose. That choice belongs to you and your surgeon. Prior LASIK, PRK, or RK does not automatically rule out a lens type. You can use this page at any point before the lens is implanted — before you see your surgeon, after, or as a second look.

At a glance

  • Tell every cataract surgeon if you had LASIK, PRK, or RK, even if it was decades ago. Bring any old records you still have.
  • These procedures change the shape of the cornea, so the usual methods of choosing lens power are less accurate.
  • Surgeons can use specialized methods designed for eyes after this kind of surgery. Even then, landing close to the planned target is less consistent than in eyes that have not had that surgery.
  • RK is different from LASIK and PRK. After RK, some people's prescription drifted toward farsightedness over the years, and vision can still change a little from morning to evening.
  • If the focus lands off the planned target, options can include glasses or contacts, a laser touch-up on the cornea, an adjustment if you have a light-adjustable lens, or, rarely, exchanging the lens.

Why prior LASIK, PRK, or RK matters

The cornea is the front window of the eye. LASIK, PRK, and RK all change that surface so that light focuses differently. When it is later time for cataract surgery, the natural lens is replaced with an artificial one, and the power of that new lens is chosen from measurements of the eye — including the cornea. Because the cornea has already been changed, those measurements are harder to use in the usual way.

How the lens power is chosen

Before surgery, your surgeon measures the length of your eye, the curve and shape of the cornea, your current glasses prescription, and sometimes pupil size. Those numbers are used to choose the lens power.

After LASIK, PRK, or RK, two things get in the way. The true focusing power of the changed cornea is harder to measure. Some formulas also use corneal power to estimate where the new lens will sit in the eye, and when that estimate is off, the calculated lens power can be off too.

Your surgeon may use specialized formulas, or more than one method, designed for eyes after this kind of surgery. Even with those methods, the result is less consistent than in eyes that have not had corneal refractive surgery.

In a 2021 review of many studies, lens-power calculations were less accurate in eyes after LASIK, PRK, or RK. Even the better methods in most studies usually landed within half a diopter of the planned target in fewer than three-quarters of eyes after LASIK or PRK, and results varied widely from one study to the next. Sources: Wang and Koch, Ophthalmology, 2021, PMID 32615201; Wang and Koch, Taiwan Journal of Ophthalmology, 2022, PMID 35399961.

Bring your current glasses and, if you've had LASIK, PRK, or RK, any records you still have. If you wear contact lenses, your surgeon may ask you to stop wearing them for a period before the measurements. Old records can help, but they are not always required, and many people no longer have them. Ask your surgeon what is useful to bring.

LASIK and PRK

LASIK and PRK use a laser to reshape the corneal surface. The planning issue is the same idea for both: the cornea no longer has its original curve, so the usual calculations are less reliable. This can be true after surgery for nearsightedness or for farsightedness, though the details of the calculation differ. Ask your surgeon which methods they use for a cornea like yours, and how close they expect to land to the planned target.

RK is a little different

RK is older than LASIK. It used radial incisions in the cornea rather than a laser to reshape the surface. That history can make planning for cataract surgery less predictable for two further reasons.

First, some people's prescription slowly drifted toward farsightedness in the years after RK. In the 10-year PERK study, 43% of eyes drifted toward farsightedness by 1.00 diopter or more between 6 months and 10 years after RK. That long, slow change is one reason today's measurements can be harder to interpret, especially if old records are missing. This page cannot say whether a change like that is still happening in your eyes.

Source: Waring, Lynn, and McDonnell, 1994, PMID 7945032.

Second, even many years later, some people still notice that their vision changes a little from morning to evening. In a PERK follow-up of people who already noticed that change, the average shift from morning to evening, about 10 years after RK, was 0.36 diopters toward nearsightedness. Ask your surgeon whether this still applies to you, and whether measuring at more than one time of day would help.

Source: Kemp et al., 1999, PMID 10404364. That follow-up looked at 32 eyes of 20 people who already noticed daytime changes, not at everyone who had RK.

If the focus lands off the planned target

With any of the three lens categories, the final focus can land a little off the planned target. That risk is higher when lens power is harder to predict. Options include glasses or contacts, a laser touch-up on the cornea, an adjustment if you have a light-adjustable lens, or, rarely, exchanging the lens. Ask your surgeon: if I land off target, what are my options?

A light-adjustable lens is a monofocal technique, not its own category. It is designed for one distance, like other monofocals, with the option to fine-tune the power after surgery. Once your eye has healed, a special UV light treatment adjusts the lens power, and two final treatments lock it in. It takes several extra visits. You'll wear specified UV-protective glasses during all waking hours from surgery until 24 hours after the final lock-in. Some medicines and eye conditions rule it out, so your surgeon will check. Some people ask about this when power is harder to predict. This page does not tell you whether it is a fit for you. For a fuller explanation of the process, the trial results, and who may not be a fit, see The light-adjustable lens at cataract surgery.

Source: Light Adjustable Lens professional labeling, P160055, Indications, Contraindications, and UV-protective eyewear.

The same framework for every lens type

Prior LASIK, PRK, or RK does not automatically rule out a lens type. Some lens types need very precise calculations, which can be less reliable after these procedures. Each category below uses the same three prompts: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and a question to take to your surgeon. This page does not rank the categories.

Monofocal

What it is designed to do
One chosen focus — far, arm's length, or near.
Trade-offs
You will still need glasses for the distances the monofocal is not set for. After prior LASIK, PRK, or RK, that chosen focus is also a little harder to land exactly.
A question to ask
After my LASIK, PRK, or RK, how close do you expect a monofocal to land to the planned target, and what if it doesn't?

EDOF

What it is designed to do
One focus stretched from far into arm's length. Readers are still common for small print.
Trade-offs
Some people notice halos, glare, or a dim-light change. This design also depends on accurate power, which can be harder after prior corneal surgery.
A question to ask
For the EDOF lenses you use, how does my prior LASIK, PRK, or RK change what I should expect, including if the focus lands off target?

Multifocal (full-range)

What it is designed to do
More than one focus — far, arm's length, and near.
Trade-offs
Halos and glare are more common with multifocal lenses. These lenses also need very precise calculations, which can be less reliable after prior LASIK, PRK, or RK. That does not automatically rule them out.
A question to ask
For the multifocal lenses you use, is my cornea a reasonable fit, and what should I expect if the power lands off the planned target?

Questions to bring to your surgeon

  1. I had LASIK, PRK, or RK. How does that change how you choose the lens power?
  2. Which methods do you use for eyes like mine, and how close do you usually land to the planned target?
  3. Should I bring old records, and do you want measurements at more than one time of day (especially after RK)?
  4. Does this history change which lens types are a reasonable fit for my cornea?
  5. If the focus lands off the planned target, what are my options?
  6. Does anything else about my eyes change this picture?

Frequently asked questions

Does prior LASIK change my lens options?

It can change the planning. Tell every cataract surgeon you had LASIK, PRK, or RK. The history does not automatically rule out a lens type, but some types need very precise calculations, which can be less reliable after these procedures. Ask your surgeon how this applies to your cornea and to each type you are considering.

Why is lens power harder to predict after LASIK, PRK, or RK?

These procedures change the shape of the cornea, so the usual measurements and formulas are less accurate. Surgeons can use specialized methods, but even then, landing close to the planned target is less consistent. Ask your surgeon which methods they use for eyes like yours.

Should I bring my old LASIK or RK records?

Yes, if you still have them. Bring your current glasses too. Old records can help, but many people no longer have them, and they are not always required. Ask your surgeon what is useful to bring.

Is RK different from LASIK?

Yes, in some ways that matter for planning. RK used incisions rather than a laser to reshape the cornea. After RK, some people's prescription drifted toward farsightedness over the years, and some people still notice that their vision changes a little from morning to evening. Ask your surgeon how that applies to you.

Can I still get a multifocal or EDOF lens after LASIK?

Prior LASIK, PRK, or RK does not automatically rule out a lens type. Multifocal and EDOF lenses need very precise calculations, which can be less reliable after these procedures. Whether your cornea is a reasonable fit is a question for your surgeon, based on your exam. This page cannot answer that for you.

What if the focus lands off the planned target?

With any of the three lens categories, the final focus can land a little off the planned target. That risk is higher when lens power is harder to predict. Options include glasses or contacts, a laser touch-up on the cornea, an adjustment if you have a light-adjustable lens, or, rarely, exchanging the lens. Ask your surgeon what your options would be.

Does ClarityIQ tell me which lens to choose?

No. ClarityIQ explains why prior LASIK, PRK, or RK changes the planning, and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
