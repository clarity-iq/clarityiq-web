Monovision with cataract surgery

By Martin de la Presa, MD, Founder, ClarityIQ · board-certified ophthalmologist

Last updated November 23, 2026.

What monovision is designed to do

Monovision is a setup, usually with monofocal lenses, not a lens category. One eye is set for distance and the other for near. Together, the two eyes cover more distances than either eye would on its own. A monofocal can be aimed at far, arm's length, or near; monovision uses that fact in a planned way across the two eyes.

You may still need glasses for some tasks, such as night driving or long reading. This applies whether you want low dependence on glasses or you are fine wearing them for some distances. The setup does not change the basic design of a monofocal lens: each eye is still aimed at one chosen focus.

Mini-monovision

In a milder version called mini-monovision, the second eye is set for arm's length rather than for near. The difference in focus between the two eyes is smaller. Some people find that easier to adapt to; some still prefer a larger difference, or no difference at all. Ask your surgeon what those options would mean for the distances you use most, and what you might still need glasses for.

Trying it first

If you've never tried monovision, ask whether you can test it with contact lenses before surgery. A contact-lens trial can give you a sense of how the setup feels for daily tasks, including walking stairs, night driving, and time at a screen. A contact lens and an implanted lens are not the same, so a trial is only an approximation of life after surgery. Some people adjust easily and some never do; a trial is one way to find out how you respond before the lens is implanted.

Depth perception

Depth perception may be lower than with both eyes set for the same distance. Some people adjust easily and some never do. One study found depth perception decreased as the difference in focus between the two eyes increased. That is one reason surgeons often discuss a contact-lens trial, and why mini-monovision is sometimes considered as a milder setup.

Source: Hayashi et al., Journal of Refractive Surgery, 2011, PMID 20839664. Depth perception may be lower than with both eyes set for the same distance. Some people adjust easily and some never do; if you've never tried it, ask whether you can test it with contact lenses before surgery.

The same framework for the options people often compare

People whose goal is low dependence on glasses often discuss monovision, an EDOF lens, or a full-range (multifocal) lens. Which of those is even on the table depends on which distances matter most to you, how you feel about halos, glare and dim-light contrast, and what your exam allows. None of these options is ranked here. Each block uses the same three prompts: what it is designed to do, the trade-offs, and a question to take to your surgeon.

Monovision (usually with monofocal lenses)

What it is designed to do
One eye set for far and the other for near, or for arm's length in mini-monovision, so the two eyes cover more distances.
Trade-offs
Depth perception may be lower than with both eyes set for the same distance. You may still need glasses for some tasks, such as night driving or long reading. Some people adjust easily and some never do.
A question to ask
Would monovision work for me, and can I try it with contact lenses first?

EDOF

What it is designed to do
One focus stretched from far into arm's length, usually in both eyes. Readers are still common for small print.
Trade-offs
The trade-offs of EDOF lenses still apply, including some halos, glare, or a dim-light change for some people.
A question to ask
How would an EDOF lens compare with monovision for my goals and my eyes?

Multifocal (full-range)

What it is designed to do
More than one focus in each eye — far, arm's length, and near.
Trade-offs
Halos and glare are more common with multifocal lenses. In the Cochrane review, people with multifocal lenses were less likely to need glasses than people with monovision, but more likely to report glare.
A question to ask
How would a multifocal lens compare with monovision for glasses dependence and for glare?

Source for the multifocal–monovision comparison: de Silva et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2016, PMID 27943250.

If your eye has meaningful astigmatism, a toric version of a monofocal lens is one way that blur is sometimes treated, including when monovision is being discussed. See Toric lenses and astigmatism at cataract surgery. Other eye conditions can also change which options are a reasonable fit. Questions to bring to your consult

  1. Would monovision work for me, and can I try it with contact lenses first?
  2. Would you set the second eye for near, or for arm's length (mini-monovision)?
  3. With monovision, what would I still need glasses for?
  4. How would monovision compare with an EDOF or multifocal lens for my goals — including depth perception, night driving, and glare?
  5. Does anything about my eyes change this picture?

Frequently asked questions

What is monovision in cataract surgery?

Monovision is a setup, usually with monofocal lenses, not a lens category. One eye is set for distance and the other for near, or sometimes for arm's length. Together, the two eyes cover more distances. You may still need glasses for some tasks.

What is mini-monovision?

Mini-monovision is a milder version of the same setup. The second eye is set for arm's length rather than for near, so the difference in focus between the two eyes is smaller. Ask your surgeon what that would mean for the distances you use most.

Can I try monovision before surgery?

If you've never tried it, ask whether you can test it with contact lenses before surgery. A trial can give you a sense of how the setup feels, but a contact lens and an implanted lens are not the same. Some people adjust easily and some never do.

Will monovision affect depth perception?

Depth perception may be lower than with both eyes set for the same distance. One study found depth perception decreased as the difference in focus between the two eyes increased. Some people adjust easily and some never do. Ask your surgeon how that applies to you.

Will I still need glasses with monovision?

You may still need glasses for some tasks, such as night driving or long reading. What you would still need glasses for depends on how the two eyes are set and on your surgeon's exam. This page cannot answer that for you.

Does ClarityIQ tell me whether to choose monovision?

No. ClarityIQ explains what monovision is designed to do and the trade-offs to weigh, so you can have a better conversation with your surgeon. You and your surgeon make the choice together.
